**Pool service software is a weekly route system first and a billing system second. It has to hold repeating stop schedules, route density by day, chemical readings per visit, photo proof, equipment history per pool, and exception work orders. Invoicing is the output of that record, not the reason you buy.**

Most software shopping for pool companies starts in the wrong place. Someone gets tired of chasing payments, searches for billing tools, and buys whatever sends the cleanest statement. Six months later the statements are fine and the route is still a mess: techs skipping stops nobody logged, repair quotes dying in text threads, and no answer when a customer asks why their spa was green on a week you billed them.

## What a pool route actually asks of software

**A pool route is not a dispatch board. Most stops are pre assigned, repeat weekly, and are never booked by the customer. The software has to hold a recurring template per account, generate each week automatically, absorb skips and rain days without breaking the sequence, and still produce a defensible record of every stop.**

Compare that to an emergency trade. A plumbing dispatcher fills tomorrow from an empty board. A pool dispatcher starts with tomorrow already 90 percent full and only decides where the exceptions fit. The tools built around inbound call handling, like the approaches covered in [Technician Dispatch Software: The View From the Truck](/guides/technician-dispatch-software/), solve a different problem than a fixed Tuesday route in one subdivision.

### Recurring stops versus exception visits

Your week has two kinds of work. Maintenance stops are templated, priced on a flat monthly rate, and measured in minutes per pool. Exception visits are green pool recoveries, filter changes, pump swaps, heater diagnostics, and callbacks. If your software treats both as generic jobs, exception work quietly eats route time and never gets separately costed. You want the two tracked as distinct job types from the start so you can see how many exception hours your maintenance routes are absorbing for free.

## Route density is the margin lever

**Route density decides your gross margin more than pricing does. A tech who runs 20 tight stops earns on 20 stops of drive time paid once. A tech running the same 20 stops scattered across three zips burns an hour or more in the truck and finishes late. Software should show density before you accept a new account.**

The practical test: when a new customer signs up, can the system tell you which existing day already passes within a few minutes of that address? If the answer is "the office manager remembers," you are one resignation away from losing your routing logic.

## Chemical readings and service proof

**Every stop should leave behind a structured record, not a checkbox. Free chlorine, pH, alkalinity, cyanuric acid, salt, and water temperature belong in numeric fields tied to that pool and date. Photos and a timestamp close the loop. That record is what settles a billing dispute in one screen instead of a phone argument.**

Free text notes look flexible and are useless three months later. Numeric fields let you chart a pool trending toward trouble, spot the account where a tech has logged identical readings for eight straight weeks, and hand a homeowner a real history when they claim service was skipped.

A workable per stop capture checklist:

- Arrival and departure timestamps, captured automatically, not typed
- Chemical readings in fixed numeric fields with valid ranges
- Chemicals added, with quantity, so product cost lands on the job
- At least one photo of the water and one of the equipment pad
- Skip reason codes when a stop is not serviced: locked gate, dog out, weather, customer request
- Any condition worth a follow up quote, flagged as a lead rather than buried in notes

Skip codes matter more than they look. A locked gate that gets coded and photographed is a defensible non visit. The same gate with no record is a credit you will end up issuing.

## Equipment history is where the repair revenue hides

**Maintenance revenue is capped by your route hours. Repair and replacement revenue is not. That upside only exists if the system stores pump, filter, heater, and automation details per pool, with install dates, warranty status, and past repairs, so an aging heater becomes a scheduled conversation instead of an emergency.**

This is the same asset record logic that drives service agreements in other trades. The reasoning in [HVAC CRM Software: The Equipment Record That Drives Renewals](/guides/hvac-crm-software/) applies directly to a pool pad: when the equipment is a tracked object with a service history, the next sale is visible before the failure.

Practically, ask a vendor to show you a customer record and count how many clicks it takes to see the pump model, its install date, and the last three repairs. If that takes more than two clicks or lives in an attachment, the record will not be maintained by a tech standing in the sun.

## Billing should fall out of the route record

**Recurring monthly billing, per visit charges, chemical pass through, and repair invoices should all generate from what the route already recorded. If your team retypes visit data into an invoice, you have two systems of record and they will disagree. Billing accuracy is a routing and documentation outcome.**

Get the sequence right: templated route generates the week, techs complete stops with readings and photos, exception visits and parts attach to the same account, then the month closes automatically with maintenance billed on schedule and repairs billed on completion. ### A four step evaluation framework

1. **Route test.** Load one real week for one tech. Can you generate it from templates, reorder stops by drag, and see drive time change live?
2. **Reading test.** Complete a stop on a phone in under 90 seconds with all readings, chemicals used, and two photos.
3. **Exception test.** Convert an on site finding into a quoted repair, approve it, and schedule it without leaving the customer record.
4. **Close test.** Run a month end billing cycle in the trial data and confirm every maintenance charge, chemical charge, and repair invoice traces back to a logged visit.

## When this is more system than you need

**Below roughly 60 to 80 accounts on one truck, a full route platform can cost more in setup and monthly fees than it returns. A shared calendar, a consistent photo folder per pool, and a simple recurring invoice may be enough until a second tech makes tribal knowledge unsafe.**

The honest trigger for buying is the second route, not the first. One owner operator holds gate codes, dog names, and equipment quirks in their head and is right most of the time. The moment someone else runs those stops, that knowledge has to live somewhere a stranger can read at 7 a.m. Buy when handoff risk is real, not when a competitor posts about their software.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need pool specific software or will general field service software work?

General field service software handles scheduling and invoicing fine, but most of it has no native chemical reading fields and no per pool equipment record. You end up rebuilding those as custom fields, which techs skip. If readings and water chemistry history matter to your service claims, pool specific tooling usually wins.

### How should skipped stops be handled in billing?

Decide the policy first, then enforce it in software. Most flat rate contracts bill the month regardless of a single locked gate visit, provided the skip is coded and photographed. Weather closures on seasonal accounts are the common exception. The software's job is producing the evidence, not deciding the policy.

### Can one system handle both maintenance routes and repair jobs?

Yes, and it should, but only if the two are separate job types sharing one customer and equipment record. Track repair labor and parts against the job so you can see true repair margin instead of blending it into route revenue.

### What is the fastest thing to fix if I only change one process?

Structured chemical readings with timestamps and photos. It removes the most common source of customer disputes, gives you a real service history per pool, and makes every downstream billing question answerable in one screen.

### How long does migrating a route to new software take?

Plan for a full billing cycle. Account import and payment setup move quickly, but route templates, equipment records, and gate access notes usually need a tech to verify them stop by stop over one complete pass of the route.
