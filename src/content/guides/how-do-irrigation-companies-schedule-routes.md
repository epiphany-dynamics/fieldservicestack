**Irrigation companies schedule routes by clustering jobs into geographic zone maps, setting appointment lengths from controller and service history, and batching work inside weather windows. Because spring start-ups, fall winterizations, and mid-season repairs all compete for the same technicians, the route board is really a seasonal capacity plan, not a calendar.**

## How irrigation route scheduling actually works

**Most irrigation dispatchers build the day from three inputs: where each job sits on the zone map, what the controller and service history say about that system, and which weather windows allow the work. Jobs get grouped by neighborhood, sequenced around water access and daylight, and padded for the repairs that start-up visits almost always uncover.**

The irrigation calendar has two cliffs. Spring start-ups compress into a few weeks after freeze risk passes, and winterization blowouts compress into a few weeks before the first hard freeze. Between those cliffs, mid-season calls arrive at a steady drip: broken rotors, stuck valves, cut wires from aeration, controllers that lost programming after a power surge.

Every one of those stops lands on the same route board and pulls from the same pool of technicians. So scheduling is a triage problem as much as a mapping problem. Companies that stay on time through October treat each job type differently instead of booking everything first come, first served.

## Start with zone maps, not ZIP codes

**Zone maps are hand-drawn service areas built around job density, drive time, and crew territory, not postal boundaries. A good irrigation zone holds enough stops to fill a truck day with minimal windshield time, and each technician works the same zones all season so knowledge of individual systems compounds instead of resetting every visit.**

### Draw zones around density, not boundaries

Pull last season's customer list, drop every address on a map, and look for clusters. A practical illustrative target is a zone where a technician can complete 8 to 12 stops with under an hour of total driving. Where clusters are thin, merge neighborhoods. Where they are dense, split by day of week so Monday's zone and Tuesday's zone share a border you can flex across when one day runs long.

### Sequence stops inside each zone

Order the day's stops around logistics, not just distance. Start-ups that need the homeowner home to access the controller in the garage go in confirmed time windows. Blowouts where the tech only needs the outdoor shutoff and backflow can run in any order, so those become flex stops that absorb overruns. Gate codes, HOA access hours, and dogs belong in the customer record so sequencing does not depend on the dispatcher's memory.

## Let controller history set the appointment length

**A start-up on a documented six-zone system takes far less time than one on a neglected 24-zone property with cut wires and a discontinued controller. Pull the controller model, zone count, prior repair notes, and flagged photos before booking, then set the slot length from that record instead of one flat duration for every visit.**

Flat 60-minute slots are where irrigation schedules go to die. The fix is job-type durations tuned by history. The table below uses illustrative planning durations; time your own jobs for a season and replace them with your numbers.

| Visit type | Illustrative slot length | Scheduling rule |
|---|---|---|
| Spring start-up, residential | 45 to 75 minutes | Book in confirmed windows; allow repair add-on time |
| Winterization blowout | 30 to 60 minutes | Batch tightly by zone; one compressor per truck |
| Mid-season repair call | 60 to 120 minutes | Triage by water loss and property damage risk |
| Controller replacement | 45 to 90 minutes | Pair with programming and Wi-Fi setup |
| Backflow test | 20 to 40 minutes | Stack onto nearby stops on the same route |

Controller history matters most during start-up season. If last fall's notes say a zone three valve was weeping, that start-up gets a longer slot and a truck stocked with the right valve. The repair gets done in one visit instead of generating a callback that burns a slot next week.

## Build weather windows into the dispatch board

**Irrigation work is gated by weather in both directions: start-ups wait for the last freeze, blowouts race the first one, and rain or lightning can erase an entire route day. Dispatchers who pre-plan hold days, make-up blocks, and freeze-date triggers recover in hours instead of rebuilding the week from voicemails.**

Treat weather as a scheduling input with rules, not a surprise. A workable checklist:

- Set freeze-date triggers. Open winterization booking roughly four to six weeks before your area's average first hard freeze, and open spring start-up booking when the forecast clears overnight freeze risk.
- Reserve one make-up block per crew per week during both peak seasons. It stays empty until weather claims a day.
- Define rain rules in writing. Controller swaps and indoor estimates can proceed in light rain; valve work in saturated turf cannot.
- Set a lightning stop-work threshold and a same-morning customer notification template with a rebooking link.
- Watch extended forecasts during blowout season and compress routes south to north if your region freezes progressively.

## Balance start-ups, winterizations, and service calls

**The seasonal surges are predictable, so reserve capacity for them instead of letting the phone book them. Block most truck hours for start-ups and winterizations during their windows, hold one floating slot per crew per day for mid-season emergencies, triage everything else by consequence, and give overflow customers honest dates.**

A simple decision framework for the board:

1. **Reserve seasonal blocks first.** Six to eight weeks before each season, pre-sell start-up or winterization slots to last year's customers by text and email. Filled blocks tell you your real capacity before the phone rush starts.
2. **Hold emergency capacity.** Keep one unbooked slot per technician per day during mid-season. The gushing main line or the valve flooding a window well takes it; nobody bumps a booked customer.
3. **Triage by consequence.** Active water loss and property damage outrank a mis-aimed rotor or a dry zone on a dormant lawn.
4. **Manage overflow honestly.** Offer a confirmed date two weeks out rather than a vague promise to fit someone in. Vague promises become angry callbacks.

Illustrative capacity math: if a technician averages 10 blowouts a day and 400 winterization customers want a four-week window, one crew covers about 200 in 20 working days. You need two crews, or you need to open booking earlier and stretch the window to six weeks.

## When tight route optimization backfires

**Dense route packing fails when job durations are guesses. New territories without service history, technicians still learning systems, and start-up season repairs all blow up optimized routes. If your records are thin, schedule fewer stops per day and let the data catch up before tightening the map further.**

The callback math is the quiet killer. A technician racing 14 stops skips the slow zone walk, misses the weeping valve, and the callback next week costs a full slot plus drive time, far more than the 10 minutes saved. Over-optimization also assumes every customer record is accurate, and irrigation records rarely are after a busy season.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many stops should an irrigation technician run per day?

It depends on the job mix. Common illustrative planning ranges are 8 to 12 start-ups or 10 to 14 blowouts per technician per day in a dense zone. Mid-season repair days run fewer, often 4 to 6. Time your own jobs for a season and schedule from your actuals, not from industry folklore.

### When should winterization routes open for booking?

Open booking four to six weeks before your area's average first hard freeze, and start pre-selling at the spring start-up visit. Waiting until the forecast shows frost compresses 400 customers into a two-week panic your crews cannot absorb.

### Should technicians quote repairs during a start-up visit?

Small fixes from truck stock, like nozzles, rotors, and spray heads, should be done and invoiced on the spot. Larger work like valve replacement or wire tracing gets photographed, quoted on site, and booked into a mid-season slot so the start-up route survives the day.

### What scheduling features matter most in irrigation software?

Prioritize map-based zone routing, job-type durations instead of flat slots, recurring seasonal campaigns for start-ups and winterizations, mass rescheduling for weather days, and a per-customer system record that holds controller model, zone count, and repair photos.

### How do you recover the schedule after a rain day?

Fill the pre-held make-up blocks first, push non-urgent tune-ups before touching booked start-ups or winterizations, and send same-morning notifications with self-serve rebooking links. Recovery should take the dispatcher an hour, not a full day on the phone.
