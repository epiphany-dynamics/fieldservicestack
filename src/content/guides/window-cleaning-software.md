**Window cleaning software earns its cost when it handles the five things a general dispatch tool treats as afterthoughts: recurring-route planning, per-property service records, crew assignment and pay tracking, photo-based proof of service, and billing that splits one-time quoted jobs from monthly route invoices. Evaluate every demo against those five workflows.**

A window cleaning business does not run on new tickets. It runs on the same storefronts, offices, and homes serviced on a cycle, week after week, with rain days, skips, and callbacks layered on top. That shape of work breaks generic scheduling tools in predictable places, and it is where your evaluation should start.

## What window cleaning software actually has to handle

**A window cleaning operation runs on repeat visits to the same addresses. Software must store pane counts, access notes, and equipment requirements per property, rebuild the route every cycle, capture before and after photos, and invoice monthly plans separately from one-time storefront quotes or post-construction cleanups.**

Picture an operator running 40 storefronts on a biweekly route, 120 residential homes on quarterly service, and a steady trickle of one-time construction cleanups. The storefront manager wants a text when the crew finishes. The residential customers want a reminder the day before. The cleanup jobs need a written quote with photos attached before anyone approves a dollar.

That is three different workflows sharing one calendar. A tool built for plumbers or HVAC techs assumes every job starts with a new request. Window cleaning assumes the opposite: the job already exists, and the software's job is to make sure it happens on time, gets documented, and gets billed without anyone touching it.

## Recurring route planning is the feature to test hardest

**The route engine decides whether the software fits. In the demo, reschedule a rain day across a 60-stop week, re-sequence stops by geography, and push a skipped storefront into the next visit without double billing. If those take manual workarounds, you are looking at a scheduler, not route software.**

### Cadence and route density

Routes make money through density. Ten stops in one neighborhood beat twelve stops spread across three. Good route tools let you group recurring stops by zone and day, then show you the gaps. When a new storefront signs on, you should be able to see which existing route day it belongs on instead of guessing from a map.

This is the same logic that drives weekly service trades. The route-record approach described in [Pool Service Software: Run the Weekly Route Record](/guides/pool-service-software/) applies almost line for line: cadence first, geography second, and a record per stop that the next visit builds on.

### Weather days, skips, and callbacks

Rain is a scheduling event, not an excuse. When Tuesday washes out, the software should shift that day's route block forward, notify affected customers, and keep the billing cycle intact. A customer who skips a visit should roll to the next cycle cleanly, with the skip logged so nobody invoices for work that did not happen.

Callbacks matter too. A streaked pane complaint three days after service should open against the original visit, with the crew's photos attached, so you can see whether the claim holds before sending someone back for free.

## Property records and proof of service

**Every repeat address needs a living record: pane count by elevation, screen and track notes, gate codes, dogs, customer preferences, and a photo log. Proof of service protects you when a property manager disputes a storefront visit or a homeowner claims a scratch. Timestamped crew photos settle most disputes before they become refunds.**

The property record is what separates a route business from a route headache. When a new tech covers a route, they should open the stop and see everything the last tech knew: which side has the hard water staining, that the back gate sticks, that the customer only wants exterior glass this quarter.

Proof of service has two audiences. Commercial property managers want confirmation the visit happened, often a timestamped photo set emailed automatically. Residential customers rarely ask, but the photos are what end the argument when someone claims a sill was damaged. Without them, you are refunding on trust.

## Crews, time tracking, and billing

**Window cleaning crews often mix hourly and per-stop pay, and billing splits between monthly route invoices and one-time quoted jobs. The software should show which crew hit which stops, flag missed stops before end of day, and generate route invoices in a batch instead of one at a time after a 40-stop week.**

### Crew assignment and pay visibility

With two or three crews running separate routes, the office needs a live view: which stops are done, which are pending, and which got skipped and why. If a tech marks a storefront as skipped because the manager asked them to come back Thursday, that note should travel with the stop, not live in a text message.

Pay visibility matters if you compensate per stop or per pane. Even if the software does not run payroll, it should export the stop-level completion data your pay calculation depends on. ### Batch invoicing and the cash gap

Route invoicing is a volume problem. After a 40-stop week, nobody should be creating 40 invoices by hand. Look for batch generation by route and cycle, plus automatic card charging for customers on file. One-time jobs flow the other direction: quote first, convert on approval, invoice on completion.

## Where generic tools fall short, and when they are enough

**Honest limitation: a solo operator with 30 recurring residential stops and no crews may not need dedicated route software yet. A general field service tool with solid reminders and invoicing can carry a small route comfortably. Route rebuild time and missed-stop errors are the signals that justify the switch, not a feature list.**

The failure point is usually administrative time, not capability. As an illustrative assumption, somewhere around 100 to 150 recurring stops is where manually rebuilding routes each cycle, chasing skips, and hand-invoicing starts costing a day a week. Below that, the overhead of a heavier platform can cost more than it saves. Above that, the missed stops and billing leaks cost more than the subscription.

Do not buy crew management and route density analytics for a one-person operation. Buy for the business you will run in 18 months, not the one in the brochure.

## How to evaluate options in a demo week

**Score every option during a live trial week on your real route, not a sales call with sample data. Load 20 actual stops, run a weather reschedule, batch-invoice one route day, and have a tech complete stops from the field app. The tool that handles your Tuesday is the tool that fits.**

1. Load 15 to 20 real recurring stops with their actual cadence, zones, and property notes.
2. Simulate a rain day: shift the route, send the notifications, confirm billing stays clean.
3. Have a tech complete five stops from the mobile app, with photos and a skip reason.
4. Batch-invoice one route day and quote one hypothetical cleanup job.
5. Export stop-level completion data and check whether it supports your pay method.
6. Score each step as easy, workaround, or impossible. Two impossible scores ends the trial.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between window cleaning software and general field service software?

General field service tools assume each job starts with a new request. Window cleaning software assumes the job already exists on a recurring cycle. The practical differences are route rebuilding, skip handling, per-property service records, and batch invoicing for repeat visits.

### Can window cleaning software handle both residential and commercial work?

Most platforms can, but test the split in a demo. Commercial route stops need proof-of-service delivery to property managers, while residential stops need reminders and easy card payments. The billing side should separate monthly route invoices from quoted one-time jobs without manual sorting.

### How does proof of service actually prevent disputes?

Timestamped before and after photos, captured in the crew's mobile app and attached to the stop record, give you evidence when a property manager questions whether a visit happened or a customer claims damage. Most disputes end at the photo log instead of becoming refunds.

### When should a small window cleaning business upgrade from spreadsheets?

Upgrade when route administration starts costing you a day a week, or when missed stops and skipped-visit billing errors become regular. For many operators that lands somewhere around 100 recurring stops, though crew count matters as much as stop count.

### Does window cleaning software handle per-stop or piece-rate pay?

Some platforms track stop-level completion data that supports per-stop pay calculations, and a few calculate it directly. Confirm in the demo that you can export which tech completed which stops with timestamps, because that export is what your pay method depends on.

Start with the evaluation framework above, run one real route week through your top two options, and let the rain-day test make the decision for you.
