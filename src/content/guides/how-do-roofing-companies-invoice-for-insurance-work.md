**Roofing companies invoice insurance work by billing against the adjuster's approved scope, not their own estimate. That means photo-documenting damage before tear-off, mirroring the carrier's line items on the invoice, collecting the deductible from the homeowner, and tracking supplements as separate, labeled receivables so approved add-ons never get buried in the original balance.**

## How insurance roofing invoices differ from retail invoices

**An insurance roofing job has two payers and two documents. The carrier pays the approved amount minus depreciation and deductible in stages, while the homeowner owes the deductible and any upgrades. Your invoice has to show who owes what, or collections stall while everyone assumes someone else paid.**

A retail re-roof is simple: estimate, signature, deposit, final invoice. A claim job adds a carrier estimate, a payment schedule you don't control, and a paper trail that a reviewer may read weeks later.

Here is an illustrative example. These numbers are made up to show the structure, not a benchmark:

- Approved replacement cost: $18,000
- Depreciation held back: $4,000
- Homeowner deductible: $1,500
- First carrier payment: $12,500 ($18,000 minus $4,000 minus $1,500)
- Homeowner owes: $1,500
- Recoverable depreciation, released after the carrier gets proof of completion: $4,000

That is three separate collection events on one roof. If your invoice shows a single $18,000 balance, your office can't tell whether the job is paid, partly paid, or waiting on the carrier. Split the balance into carrier payment, deductible, and held-back depreciation from day one.

## Document damage before you invoice

**Take dated photos of every slope, ridge, vent, and flashing detail before tear-off, plus close-ups of the damage the adjuster will dispute. Tag each photo to the job record. These photos support the initial claim, justify supplements later, and settle callback arguments months after the crew leaves.**

Documentation is an invoicing task, not just a sales task. The photo set is the evidence behind every dollar you bill above the first estimate.

### What the photo set should include

- Each roof slope, wide shot, labeled by direction or section
- Damage close-ups with a chalk mark or scale reference
- Every penetration: pipe boots, vents, chimney flashing, skylights
- Drip edge, valleys, and ridge before removal
- Decking condition once shingles are off, before underlayment goes down
- Finished work on each slope, including the details a leak call would hinge on

### Who takes them and when

The estimator or inspector takes the pre-work set. The crew lead takes the tear-off set, because rotted decking only shows up once the shingles are gone and the crew is the only one there. Make it a stop-work checkpoint: no underlayment until decking photos are uploaded.

The same photos protect you on callbacks. If a homeowner calls eight months later about a leak at a chimney, dated finished-work photos show whether the flashing was replaced or reused. That is a five-minute dispatch decision instead of a truck roll and an argument.

## Match invoice line items to the adjuster's scope

**Build the invoice from the carrier's estimate, using the same line items, quantities, and units, such as squares of shingle, linear feet of drip edge, and pipe boots. Adjusters and reviewers have less to question when they can compare your document to theirs line by line.**

Your internal estimate may bundle labor and materials or use your own naming. The carrier's estimate won't. If you invoice "full roof system, $18,000," the reviewer has to guess how it maps to their scope. If you invoice tear-off, felt, shingles, drip edge, and boots in their order and units, the comparison is quick.

### A line-item mapping example

Again, these figures are illustrative:

| Line item | Carrier scope | What the crew found or did | Invoice treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tear-off and disposal | 30 squares | 30 squares | Bill as scoped |
| Architectural shingles | 30 squares | 30 squares | Bill as scoped |
| Drip edge | 180 linear feet | 210 linear feet installed | 180 on original, 30 on supplement |
| Pipe boots | 4 | 5 found | 4 on original, 1 on supplement |
| Decking replacement | 0 sheets | 3 sheets rotted | Supplement with photos |

The rule: the original invoice mirrors the approved scope exactly. Anything the crew did beyond it goes on a supplement, never quietly added to a scoped line.

### Keep the homeowner's portion clear

Upgrades the homeowner chose, such as a different shingle line, are not insurance items. Put them on a separate homeowner invoice or a clearly separate section, so the carrier's payment is never applied against work it didn't approve.

## Track supplements as separate receivables

**Treat every supplement as its own receivable with its own number, reason, photos, and status. Original scope, supplement one, and supplement two should each show requested, approved, and paid amounts. Blending them hides which dollars are still owed and which were denied.**

Supplements are where insurance roofing money leaks. The crew finds hidden damage, the office intends to follow up, and three months later nobody remembers whether the $1,150 decking request was approved.

### A simple supplement ledger

| Record | Requested | Approved | Paid | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Original scope | $18,000 | $18,000 | $12,500 | Depreciation pending |
| Supplement 1: decking, drip edge | $1,150 | Pending | $0 | Submitted with photos |
| Supplement 2: extra boot | $95 | $95 | $95 | Closed |

Each row gets a submission date, the photos attached, and the name of whoever is following up. A denied supplement should stay on the ledger with the reason, because it tells you whether to adjust your estimating notes or push back.

## What your invoicing workflow needs to do

**A workable insurance invoicing process follows a fixed sequence: attach the carrier estimate, mirror its scope, split payers, log supplements separately, and close out only after depreciation is paid. Software helps only if it supports those steps without workarounds.**

Use this as a checklist when you review your process or your software:

1. Store the carrier estimate and claim number on the job record.
2. Require pre-work and tear-off photo uploads before the crew moves on.
3. Create the invoice from the approved scope, using the carrier's line items and units.
4. Split the balance into carrier payment, deductible, and recoverable depreciation.
5. Create each supplement as a separate invoice or clearly separate record.
6. Attach photos and the reason to every supplement.
7. Record each payment against the specific item it covers.
8. Send the completion documents the carrier needs to release depreciation.
9. Keep the job open until every receivable is paid or written off.

## When not to invoice through the carrier

**Skip carrier-facing invoicing when the homeowner hasn't signed a work authorization, the claim is denied, or local rules limit what a contractor may do in a claim. In those cases, bill the homeowner directly on your own estimate. Rules vary by state, so confirm yours.**

Some states restrict how contractors may discuss coverage, negotiate with adjusters, or handle deductibles. This article covers billing mechanics, not legal advice. Ask a local attorney or your state's contractor licensing board what you may and may not do.

Also be careful with depreciation. If you start work before the homeowner understands they owe the deductible and may wait on held-back funds, you become the lender. Confirm the payment schedule in writing before the crew arrives.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do roofers invoice the insurance company or the homeowner?

Usually both, for different amounts. The homeowner typically owes the deductible and any upgrades, and the carrier pays the approved balance, often to the homeowner or jointly. Your invoice should show each party's share so it is clear who owes what.

### What is a roofing supplement, and how should I invoice it?

A supplement is a request for additional payment when the actual work exceeds the adjuster's original scope, such as rotted decking found at tear-off. Invoice it separately from the original scope, with its own number, photos, reason, and status.

### Why should invoice line items match the adjuster's estimate?

Matching lets the carrier compare your bill to its own scope line by line. Differences then stand out as clearly labeled supplements instead of unexplained totals, which cuts down on back-and-forth questions.

### When is recoverable depreciation collected?

Policies differ, but the held-back depreciation is generally released after the carrier receives proof the work is complete. Check the policy and carrier instructions, then keep the job open on your books until that payment arrives.

### What photos do I need for a roofing insurance claim?

Take dated photos of every slope, all penetrations, drip edge and valleys, damage close-ups, decking after tear-off, and the finished work. Tag them to the job record so they are easy to find for supplements or later callbacks.
