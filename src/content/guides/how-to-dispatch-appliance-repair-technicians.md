**Dispatching appliance repair technicians means matching each incoming job's brand, model, and symptom to a tech's skill set and van stock before assigning a time slot. This guide covers intake data capture, skill matching, parts checks, board scoring, and the follow-up habits that protect your first-visit fix rate.**

Appliance repair is not a generic service call. A "fridge not cooling" ticket could be a $40 start relay on a ten-year-old top freezer or a sealed-system compressor job on a built-in unit that requires certification, authorization, and two techs. Dispatch it wrong and you burn a truck roll, a customer's patience, and usually the job itself.

## Capture the right job data at intake

**The dispatcher can only match what intake captured. Require brand, full model number, the symptom in the customer's own words, any error codes on the display, and photos if the customer can send them. A missing model number is the most common cause of a wasted truck roll in appliance repair.**

Train whoever answers the phone to treat the model number as non-negotiable. It lives on a sticker inside the door, behind the kick plate, or on the back of the unit, and walking the customer through finding it takes two minutes. That two minutes buys you the service manual, the parts diagram, and the failure history for that exact platform.

A good intake script captures, in order:

1. Brand and full model number, read off the rating plate
2. Symptom in plain language, plus any displayed error code
3. Age of the unit and whether it is under manufacturer or third-party warranty
4. Gas or electric, and whether the unit is built in
5. Access notes: tight laundry closet, second-floor install, pet in the home
6. Photos of the rating plate and the symptom, texted or emailed

That warranty question matters more than most shops realize. A home warranty ticket often requires an authorization number before any work starts, and skipping that step can mean the claim is denied and your shop eats the labor. Flag these tickets at intake so dispatch treats them differently from retail work.

## Match brand, model, and symptom to tech skill

**A symptom like "washer not draining" maps to a small set of failure modes per platform, and the tech who knows that platform fixes it in one visit while the generalist guesses. Build a skill matrix by brand and appliance type, then rank available techs against each ticket before assigning.**

The matrix does not need to be fancy. A spreadsheet with techs down one side and brand-plus-appliance combinations across the top works fine. Mark each cell as strong, competent, or supervised-only. Then the match becomes mechanical: a Samsung front-load throwing an ND code goes to whoever is strong on Samsung laundry, not whoever happens to be closest.

Two constraints sit outside the matrix and override it:

- **Sealed-system refrigeration work.** Any job that opens the refrigerant circuit requires a tech with EPA Section 608 certification. A "fridge warm, compressor running" symptom is a possible sealed-system job, so it never goes to an uncertified tech even for diagnosis if you expect same-visit repair.
- **Gas appliances.** Licensing rules for gas dryer and range work vary by state and municipality. Know which of your techs are cleared before the ticket hits the board, not when they are standing in the customer's kitchen.

High-end and European brands deserve their own flag. A Sub-Zero or Miele call sent to a tech who has never opened one usually ends as a diagnostic-only visit, a follow-up appointment, and a customer who expected better for a premium appliance.

## Check parts before you promise a slot

**First-visit fix rate dies at the parts shelf. Once intake and the skill match narrow the likely failure, verify whether the tech's van or the shop stocks the candidate parts. If the part is not stocked and not available from a local distributor same day, order it and schedule the visit around arrival.**

This is where appliance dispatch diverges from plumbing or HVAC dispatch. The parts universe is enormous and model-specific. You cannot stock everything, so stock by failure frequency. As an illustrative stocking logic, not a published benchmark, many shops keep cheap, high-failure universal parts on every van: gas dryer igniters and coils, washer drain pumps for the brands they see most, dishwasher inlet valves, thermal fuses, and common bake elements. A gas dryer with no heat is an igniter or coil set the large majority of the time in most operators' experience, so a tech without those parts on the van is accepting a likely callback before the day starts.

Expensive model-specific parts like control boards and sealed-system components follow the opposite rule. Diagnose first, order second, and schedule the return visit around the distributor's delivery commitment. The discipline is in the handoff: the tech records the exact part number from the unit's wiring diagram or service manual, the office orders against the model number on file, and the return appointment is not booked until the part is physically in the shop.

## Score and sequence the dispatch board

**When several techs could take a call, score each assignment instead of going by gut. Weight skill match, parts on hand, drive time, job value, and callback history. The highest combined score gets the ticket, and ties go to whichever assignment protects the most first-visit fixes across the day's board.**

An illustrative scoring rubric looks like this. Adjust the weights to your shop's reality; the structure matters more than the numbers.

| Factor | Weight | What the dispatcher checks |
|---|---|---|
| Skill match | 30 | Brand and appliance type rated strong in the tech's matrix |
| Parts on hand | 25 | Candidate failure parts on the van or in the shop |
| Drive time | 15 | Fit with the tech's existing route that day |
| Job value | 15 | Retail repair ticket versus warranty flat rate |
| Callback risk | 15 | Tech's recent callback history on that brand |

Two notes on using it. First, drive time is deliberately weighted below skill and parts. Sending the closest tech instead of the right tech is how a 20-minute drive turns into a second 40-minute round trip. Second, sequence the day so parts pickups happen on the way to the first stop, not as a dedicated trip. ## Protect the first-visit fix rate after dispatch

**Dispatch is not finished when the tech rolls. Send the customer a confirmation with the model and symptom attached, give the tech the service manual link before arrival, and require a reason code on every second visit. Those codes are the data that sharpens next month's matching decisions.**

Pre-visit confirmation does double duty: it cuts no-access callbacks and it catches intake errors while there is still time to fix them. A customer who replies "actually it is the dishwasher, not the disposal" just saved you a wasted stop. Automated confirmations and day-before texts are covered in depth in [Automated Appointment Reminders That Protect Your Dispatch Board](/guides/automated-appointment-reminders/).

On the back end, make every callback carry one of these reason codes:

- Wrong diagnosis on the first visit
- Part not stocked on the van or locally
- Correct diagnosis, wrong part ordered
- No access: customer absent or appliance unreachable
- Second, unrelated failure discovered during repair

Review the codes monthly by tech and by brand. A tech whose callbacks cluster on one brand needs matrix adjustments, not a lecture. A shop-wide cluster on "part not stocked" is a truck stock problem. ## When not to auto-dispatch appliance jobs

**Do not auto-assign jobs with missing model numbers, sealed-system symptoms, gas appliance work, or third-party warranty tickets. These carry certification, licensing, authorization, or liability constraints that a rules engine will miss. Route them to a human dispatcher who verifies credentials and parts before committing a slot.**

Automation is genuinely useful for the middle of the board: retail electric appliances, known platforms, stocked parts, flexible windows. But the four exception categories above account for a disproportionate share of denied claims, compliance exposure, and ugly callbacks. A home warranty ticket auto-dispatched without an authorization number is revenue you will never collect. A sealed-system job auto-assigned to an uncertified tech is a compliance problem, not an efficiency win. Keep a human gate on anything the rules cannot fully verify, and revisit the gate quarterly as your data improves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good first-visit fix rate for appliance repair?

Many operators treat 80 to 90 percent as a healthy target range for first-visit fix rate, counting only jobs where a completed repair was actually possible. Treat that as a common rule of thumb, not a published industry benchmark. Your own trend line matters more than any external number: track it monthly by tech and brand and act on the reason codes behind every miss.

### How many parts should a tech carry on the van?

Stock by failure frequency, not by catalog. Cheap, universal, high-failure parts for the brands you service most belong on every van. Expensive model-specific boards and sealed-system components belong in the shop, ordered after diagnosis. Audit van stock against your callback reason codes quarterly and cut whatever has not moved.

### Should the newest tech get the easiest jobs?

Only if the job is easy for a known reason. A junior tech should get tickets where the intake data is complete, the platform is common, and the candidate parts are stocked, because those are winnable first visits that build skill. Avoid using new techs as the default for vague, poorly triaged calls; that just converts your weakest intake into your weakest outcomes.

### How do I handle home warranty dispatch calls?

Treat them as a separate job type. Confirm the authorization number and the covered scope before dispatching, send a tech comfortable with flat-rate margin math and warranty paperwork, and document the diagnosis against the exact model number. Without authorization before work starts, expect the claim to be denied.
