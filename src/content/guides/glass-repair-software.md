**Glass repair software is worth buying only if it handles the full mobile auto-glass chain: damage intake with photos, VIN-based parts matching, technician dispatch with glass and adhesive on the truck, insurer-ready documentation, and same-day invoicing. Evaluate tools against that workflow, not against a generic feature checklist.**

Most auto-glass operators lose money in the gaps between systems. The CSR takes a claim number on a sticky note, the parts desk guesses at a rain-sensor bracket, the tech arrives with the wrong windshield, and the insurer kicks the invoice back for missing photos. Each gap is a callback, a reschedule, or a denied claim. This guide walks the workflow stage by stage so you can score software against the work your crews actually do.

## How a mobile auto-glass job actually flows

**A mobile auto-glass job moves through five linked stages: intake with damage photos and insurance details, parts lookup tied to the VIN, scheduling and dispatch to a stocked truck, on-site completion with documentation, and billing to the insurer or customer. Software that drops any link creates manual rework downstream.**

The chain looks like this in practice:

1. A customer or agent reports a cracked windshield. The CSR captures the VIN, damage photos, deductible, and whether calibration is needed.
2. The parts desk decodes the VIN to identify the correct glass, molding, and any ADAS camera bracket.
3. Dispatch assigns a technician whose van already carries that part, or schedules around a warehouse pickup.
4. The tech completes the install, captures pre- and post-photos, gets a signature, and notes safe drive-away time.
5. The office submits the invoice with the documentation packet to the insurer, or collects payment on-site.

Every stage feeds the next. A bad intake poisons the parts order. A bad parts order wastes a dispatch slot. A thin documentation packet delays payment by weeks. When you demo software, walk a real job through all five stages instead of watching a canned tour.

## Damage intake and parts matching

**Intake is where glass repair software earns or loses its price. The system should capture VIN, photos, insurance claim numbers, and vehicle options at booking, then decode the VIN into an exact part specification. If your CSRs still re-key VINs into a separate parts catalog, you are paying for two systems and a transcription error.**

Windshield variants are the classic failure. A 2022 SUV might have four windshield SKUs depending on lane-keep cameras, heated wipers, acoustic lamination, and heads-up display. The wrong glass means a wasted 45-minute drive and a rescheduled customer who now doubts you.

Test these intake capabilities during evaluation:

- VIN decode that returns glass part numbers or at least a short list of option-dependent variants.
- Photo upload at booking, so the tech sees crack location and size before rolling.
- Insurance fields for carrier, claim number, and deductible, captured once and carried through to invoicing.
- A flag for ADAS calibration need, since recalibration changes job duration and sometimes requires a partner or a follow-up visit.

A practical test: take your last ten jobs where the wrong part shipped, and replay them in the demo. If the software would not have caught at least eight, the intake module is not doing its job. ## Technician dispatch for mobile glass work

**Mobile auto-glass dispatch has one constraint most trades do not: the right part must physically be on the right truck. Good glass repair software ties the dispatch decision to van inventory, drive time, and calibration requirements, so a scheduler can see which tech can actually complete the job today.**

The scenario that exposes weak tools: a morning cancellation frees a two-hour slot, and a new chip repair call comes in across town. Can your board show which nearby tech has resin and a gap in the route, or does the dispatcher call three phones to find out? Chip repairs are 20-minute jobs that live or die on routing density, so this question matters daily.

Evaluate dispatch on these points:

- Van-level inventory, or at least a parts-assigned-to-job check before the slot is confirmed.
- Map-based scheduling that respects safe drive-away time between jobs, since a tech cannot rush a urethane cure.
- Job duration rules by work type: chip repair, full replacement, replacement plus calibration.
- Customer notifications with arrival windows, which cut no-access visits sharply.

## Insurer documentation and invoicing

**A large share of auto-glass revenue flows through insurance claims, and insurers reject or delay invoices with missing photos, signatures, or part numbers. Glass repair software should assemble the claim packet automatically from job data and photos captured on-site, then track the claim through payment instead of letting it age in a spreadsheet.**

The documentation packet for a typical replacement includes pre-existing damage photos, the installed glass part number, proof of calibration where required, a customer signature, and the invoice with the deductible split. If your techs text photos to the office and someone assembles packets by hand, you have a bottleneck that scales badly past a few jobs a day.

Score the billing module on:

| Capability | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Photos attached to the job record automatically | No hunting through text threads at billing time |
| Insurer-specific invoice formats or exports | Reduces rejections and resubmissions |
| Claim status tracking with aging alerts | Catches claims stuck at 30-plus days |
| Deductible collection on-site via card payment | Closes the cash loop before the truck leaves |
| Calibration documentation fields | Increasingly required by carriers on ADAS vehicles |

## Where glass repair software falls short

**No field service platform fully replaces the glass industry networks and pricing catalogs that insurers reference for billing. Most general tools handle scheduling, dispatch, and invoicing well but rely on integrations or manual steps for insurer rate structures, so expect a hybrid stack rather than one magic system.**

Be honest about three limitations before you buy:

- Generic FSM tools rarely include glass-specific parts catalogs. You may keep your existing catalog and need the FSM to accept part numbers from it cleanly.
- Insurer billing portals and network referral programs live outside your software. The best case is a clean export; the common case is double entry. Ask vendors exactly which carriers and networks they integrate with, in writing.
- ADAS calibration documentation requirements vary by carrier and change often. A template that satisfied claims last year may not satisfy them next quarter.

If your shop is two techs doing mostly cash chip repairs, a full platform may be overkill. A simple scheduling and invoicing tool plus disciplined photo habits covers that operation. The ROI case builds at the point where insurance claims, multi-tech routing, and parts complexity start colliding.

## How to score vendors against your workflow

**Run every candidate through a scripted demo using your last month of real jobs, and score each stage of the chain separately. A tool that aces scheduling but fumbles insurer documentation will cost you more in rejected claims than it saves in dispatch time, so weight the stages by where your money actually leaks.**

Use this decision framework in the demo:

1. Intake: book a real job from a real call recording. Time it. Over four minutes is friction.
2. Parts: replay a job where the wrong glass shipped. Does the system catch it?
3. Dispatch: simulate a same-day chip repair insert. How many clicks and calls?
4. Field: have the vendor show the technician app on a phone with gloves-on usability in mind.
5. Billing: generate the claim packet for an insurance job and count what is missing.
6. Reporting: pull callbacks, claim aging, and jobs per tech per day without vendor help.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is glass repair software?

Glass repair software is field service management tooling configured for auto-glass and flat-glass work. It combines booking, VIN-based parts matching, technician dispatch, on-site documentation, and insurance claim invoicing in one system, replacing the mix of spreadsheets, phone calls, and paper work orders most shops start with.

### Does glass repair software handle ADAS calibration documentation?

Better platforms include fields for calibration type, method, and results attached to the job record, and some integrate with calibration scan tools. Because carrier requirements vary and change, verify that the software lets you customize documentation templates rather than locking you into a fixed form.

### Can general field service software work for auto glass?

Yes, with caveats. General tools handle scheduling, dispatch, and invoicing well. The gaps are glass-specific parts catalogs and insurer billing formats, which usually require integrations or manual steps. For a shop doing mostly cash retail work, a general tool is often enough.

### How much does glass repair software cost?

Pricing varies widely by vendor, seat count, and modules, so treat any specific figure as illustrative. As a rough planning assumption, small operations often budget in the low hundreds of dollars per month for a core platform, with per-user fees adding to that as the team grows. Get written quotes with your actual seat count.

### What should I test in a demo before buying?

Replay your last ten problem jobs: wrong parts, rejected claims, and missed same-day inserts. Time the intake flow, check van inventory visibility, and generate a full insurance claim packet. If the vendor cannot complete these with your real scenarios, the fit is weak regardless of the feature list.

### When is a shop too small for dedicated glass repair software?

A one- or two-truck operation doing mostly cash chip repairs can often run on simple scheduling and invoicing software plus disciplined photo habits. The dedicated platform pays off when insurance claims, multi-tech routing, and windshield variant complexity begin generating rework every week.
