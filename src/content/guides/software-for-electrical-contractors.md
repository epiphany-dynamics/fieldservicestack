**Most electrical contractors need software built for the side of the business that generates the majority of revenue, not both sides equally. Service shops need dispatch, callbacks, and same-day invoicing. Project shops need estimating, phasing, change orders, and progress billing. Count your last 90 days of revenue before shortlisting anything.**

Electrical is one of the few trades where two completely different businesses share a name. One shop runs a dispatch board, sends two vans to panel swaps and dead outlets, and closes the ticket on site. The other bids a 40-unit apartment rough-in, schedules crews across six weeks, and bills in draws. Software vendors rarely serve both well, and the demo will not tell you which one you are looking at.

## Run the 90-day revenue split before you shortlist

**Pull the last 90 days of closed revenue and split every job into two buckets: dispatched service and scheduled project work. Whichever bucket holds more than 60 percent of dollars decides your primary tool. Under 60 percent either way, you are a mixed shop and need a different buying approach.**

The diagnostic takes about 20 minutes with an invoice export:

1. Export every paid invoice from the last 90 days.
2. Tag each one **service** if it was dispatched within a week of the customer call and finished in a single visit, or **project** if it was bid in advance and spans more than two days.
3. Sum dollars per bucket. Then sum job count per bucket.
4. Compare the two ratios against each other.

The gap between those ratios is the part most owners have never looked at. Take an illustrative residential and light commercial shop: 180 service tickets averaging $410, plus 11 projects averaging $14,000. Service is 94 percent of the job count and 32 percent of the revenue. Those numbers are hypothetical, but the shape is common, and it changes the answer. That shop feels like a service shop every single day because the board is full of tickets, yet two thirds of its money comes from work the dispatch board barely touches.

The rule of thumb: buy for the dollars, not the ticket count, then make sure the tool does not actively obstruct the high-volume side.

## What service-heavy shops need from electrical software

**Service-heavy shops live and die on the dispatch board. The software has to move a tech mid-day without losing the work order, price flat-rate repairs from a synced price book, capture photos and panel readings on site, invoice before the van leaves, and link every callback to the original visit.**

The callback link is the piece buyers skip and regret. When a GFCI you replaced in March trips again in May, you need the second visit attached to the first: same customer record, same equipment, same tech, flagged as warranty so it does not read as new revenue. Without that, your callback rate is invisible and you cannot tell whether a specific tech, a specific supplier part, or a specific job type is bleeding hours.

Two other service-side requirements worth testing hard:

- **Drag-and-drop reschedule with travel awareness.** If a service call goes long, moving the next three stops should take one screen, not three edits and a phone call.
- **On-site payment that closes the ticket.** Card on the tablet, invoice emailed before the tech pulls out of the driveway, and the payment posted to accounting without a second entry.

## What project-heavy shops need instead

**Project-heavy shops need the estimate to survive contact with the field. That means assembly-based takeoff, labor units you can adjust, phased schedules that span weeks, change orders that reprice without a rebuild, progress billing with retainage, and cost codes that let you compare bid hours to actual hours per phase.**

The core difference is that a service ticket is priced once and closed. A project is priced once, then re-priced every time the GC moves a wall. If your software cannot issue a change order that carries its own labor and material lines back into the job budget, your project manager keeps a spreadsheet, and the spreadsheet becomes the real system of record.

Watch for these three gaps when a service-first platform is sold as project-capable:

- **Labor units are flat.** Real estimating tools let you set hours per device by condition. Service tools usually give you one line item and a price.
- **Scheduling is per-visit, not per-phase.** A six-week rough-in is not 30 daily appointments.
- **Billing is invoice-only.** No draw schedule, no percent complete, no retainage held back.

## Scoring a shortlist when the split is close

**Score candidates on the eight capabilities that actually break when a tool is used off-side. Weight each one by how often it touches your week, not by how impressive the demo looked. A tool that scores well on your dominant side and merely adequate on the other is the right buy.**

| Capability | Weight if service-heavy | Weight if project-heavy | Failure you will see if it is weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drag-and-drop dispatch board | High | Low | Dispatcher rebuilds the day by phone |
| Flat-rate price book | High | Low | Techs quote from memory, margins drift |
| On-site invoice and payment | High | Medium | Receivables age past 30 days |
| Callback linked to original job | High | Medium | Warranty work counted as new revenue |
| Assembly takeoff and labor units | Low | High | Estimator works in a spreadsheet |
| Multi-week phasing and crew planning | Low | High | Crew assignments live on a whiteboard |
| Change orders and progress billing | Low | High | Scope creep billed late or not at all |
| Job costing by cost code | Medium | High | You learn the job lost money at closeout |

Score each product 0 to 3 per row, multiply by weight, and compare totals. If two products land within a few points, the tiebreaker is the accounting sync, because that is the integration you cannot work around. ## Where the one-tool answer breaks down, and how to trial around it

**Sometimes no single tool wins. A shop running roughly even service and project revenue with more than a handful of techs usually ends up with a service platform plus a dedicated estimating tool, and pays an integration tax in double entry. Run the trial below before committing to either shape.**

The two-system setup is not a failure. It is a real answer for mixed shops, and the cost is honest: someone re-keys awarded projects from the estimating tool into the operations tool. Budget an hour a week for that, and make sure both systems write to the same accounting file so your job costing is not split across two truths.

Before you sign anything, run this trial:

- [ ] Load your 15 most common service repairs into the price book and time how long a tech takes to build one invoice on a phone.
- [ ] Rebuild your last completed project as a live estimate, including one change order, and check whether the budget updates.
- [ ] Move three stops on the dispatch board mid-day and confirm the customer notification actually fires.
- [ ] Run one warranty callback end to end and verify it links to the original work order.
- [ ] Sync one week of invoices to your accounting software and reconcile line by line.
- [ ] Ask a tech who dislikes new software to use it unassisted for two days.

If a product fails two or more of these on your dominant side, it is not a training problem. It is the wrong tool.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can one tool handle both electrical service calls and project work?

Some can cover both adequately, but almost none excel at both. Platforms that lead with dispatch usually treat projects as long jobs with a few extra fields. Platforms that lead with estimating usually treat service calls as tiny projects. Decide which weakness you can live with, because you will meet one of them.

### What is the fastest way to tell which side my shop lives on?

Export 90 days of paid invoices, tag each as dispatched service or bid project, and compare revenue share. If one side clears 60 percent of dollars, that side is your primary. Ticket count alone will mislead you, since service generates far more tickets per dollar than project work.

### Do project-heavy shops still need a dispatch board?

Usually yes, but a lighter one. Even a project shop takes warranty calls, punch list visits, and service work from past customers. What it does not need is a dispatch-first product priced per technician when most of those technicians are assigned to a phase for six weeks and never appear on the board.

### What should I ask on a demo to expose the weak side?

Ask the rep to build a change order on a live project and show the updated job budget, then ask them to reschedule a mid-day service call and show the customer notification. Whichever request produces a workaround, a spreadsheet export, or an offer to follow up later is the side the product does not really cover.

### When is it worth running two systems?

When neither side clears 60 percent of revenue and your estimating volume justifies a dedicated tool. The threshold is practical: if your estimator spends more than a day a week on takeoff, a real estimating package pays for itself even with the double entry. Below that, one platform and a good spreadsheet is cheaper.
