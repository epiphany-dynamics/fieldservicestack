**Plumber scheduling software earns its keep by protecting emergency capacity without idling the board. Reserve a rolling share of each day for same day calls, let booked work fill everything else, and release the hold on a fixed timer. The features that make that work are hold windows, fast drag and drop rescheduling, and automatic arrival window texts.**

## How the emergency call breaks a plumbing week

**A burst pipe does not take the next open slot, it takes the slot a tech is already standing in. One 10 a.m. emergency can push three booked jobs, generate two apology calls, and bump a water heater install to next week. The real damage is the downstream rescheduling, not the emergency call itself.**

Plumbing is unusual among the trades because the urgent work and the planned work compete for the same technician on the same morning. An electrician doing a panel upgrade knows the job is on the calendar for six hours. A plumbing shop that took four drain cleanings and a fixture swap on Tuesday has no slack when a slab leak comes in at 9:40.

Most shops respond with one of two bad defaults.

### Default one: book solid and absorb the hit

Every hour gets sold. When the emergency lands, dispatch breaks a promise to whoever is easiest to move. That customer becomes the callback, the bad review, or the person who never rebooks the annual water heater flush. You never see this cost on a revenue report because the emergency invoice looks great.

### Default two: hold the afternoon and hope

The other reaction is to block off entire afternoons for emergency intake. When the phone stays quiet, techs sit. A shop that blocks four hours a day per tech and fills half of them is paying for real idle time every week. The distinction between what dispatchers reserve and what the booking calendar shows the customer is the same tension covered in [Home Service Scheduling Software: Booking vs Dispatch](/guides/home-service-scheduling-software/), and plumbing feels it harder than most trades.

## The rolling hold: reserve capacity in hours, not in slots

**Reserve emergency capacity as a number of technician hours per day, not as fixed blocks on a calendar. Hours can shrink, move, and expire. Blocks cannot. Set the hold by day of week, attach a release time, and define in advance what fills the hold if no emergency arrives by then.**

A block on the calendar is dead space with a name. A reserved hour is a budget. The dispatcher's job becomes spending that budget, not defending a rectangle.

Three rules make this work in practice:

1. **Size the hold from your own call log**, not from a rule of thumb. Count same day emergency dispatches per weekday for the last eight weeks and take the median, not the average, so one flood week does not distort the whole plan.
2. **Attach a release time.** If the reserved hours are untouched by a set hour, dispatch fills them from a standing list.
3. **Keep a release queue ready.** Deferred maintenance, camera inspections, warranty follow ups, and quoted work waiting on customer availability all belong here.

The table below shows one way to structure the hold. The hours are illustrative, meant as a starting shape to test against your own dispatch history rather than a benchmark.

| Crew size | Reserved hours per day (illustrative) | Release time | Fills the hold if unused |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 techs | 2 hours, one tech | 1:00 p.m. | Deferred drain maintenance, quoted work |
| 4 to 6 techs | 4 to 5 hours across two techs | 12:00 p.m. | Camera inspections, water heater swaps |
| 8+ techs | One tech fully floating, plus 2 shared hours | 11:00 a.m. | Commercial PM route, backlog callbacks |
| Any size, Monday | Increase hold by roughly half | 2:00 p.m. | Same, but held later |

Monday and the day after a hard freeze both carry more emergency volume than a normal Thursday. A static hold ignores that. A hold that flexes by weekday and weather is the difference between a plan and a habit.

## Scheduling features that actually support the hold

**Four capabilities decide whether a reserved hour model survives contact with a real dispatch board: named capacity holds, one screen rescheduling, automatic customer notification on any time change, and a call intake path that captures the emergency before a dispatcher is free. Everything else is convenience.**

Vet software against this list before you look at price:

1. **Capacity holds that are visible but not customer bookable.** Online booking must not sell the reserved hours. If the tool only offers a generic calendar block, the hold gets filled by the internet at 2 a.m.
2. **Drag and drop rescheduling that recalculates drive time.** Moving three jobs after an emergency should take a dispatcher under a minute, with travel gaps updating live.
3. **Automatic arrival window texts triggered by the reschedule.** Every moved job must notify its customer without a manual call. This is the single feature that turns a bumped appointment from a complaint into a shrug.
4. **A tagged emergency job type with its own priority and rate.** If emergency work carries the same job type as a scheduled repair, you cannot report on how often the hold was actually used.
5. **Missed call capture.** Emergency callers do not leave voicemail, they call the next plumber. Automatic text back on a missed line protects the front of the funnel.
6. **A dispatch view filtered to today only.** Week views hide the thing you need to see during a scramble.

## Setting the hold Monday and auditing it Friday

**Run the model as a weekly loop, not a one time configuration. Monday morning you set the week's hold from last week's actuals and the forecast. Friday afternoon you compare reserved hours against emergency hours used, then adjust next week by one hour in whichever direction the gap points.**

The five step loop:

1. **Pull last week's emergency dispatch count by day.** Filter on the emergency job type.
2. **Set this week's reserved hours per day** from that count, adjusted for weather and holidays.
3. **Load the release queue** with enough deferred work to fill every reserved hour twice over.
4. **Track every release.** Note the time the hold was released and what filled it.
5. **Audit Friday.** If you released the hold unused more than three days out of five, cut an hour. If you broke a booked appointment more than once, add one.

## When holding capacity is the wrong move

**Reserved capacity assumes emergency demand is steady enough to plan around. A shop doing fewer than roughly two same day emergencies per week does not have a pattern to reserve against, and holding hours will cost more in idle time than it saves in broken appointments. Overflow to a subcontractor instead.**

The same applies to new construction and remodel heavy shops, where a plumber's week is committed to a general contractor's schedule and there is no discretionary capacity to hold. In both cases the better answer is a triage script at intake and a partner shop you can hand true emergencies to, keeping your own board intact. Reserved hours are a tool for shops where service volume is high enough that the emergency is predictable in aggregate even though no single call is.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much capacity should a plumbing shop hold for emergencies?

Derive it from your own dispatch log rather than a percentage rule. Count same day emergency jobs per weekday over the last eight weeks, take the median, and multiply by your average emergency job duration. That number is your starting reserved hours. Adjust it by one hour per week based on whether you released the hold unused or broke booked appointments.

### Does emergency work need a separate dispatch board?

No, and a second board usually creates more problems than it solves. What you need is a tagged emergency job type on one board so priority, rate, and reporting all follow the tag. Trade specific dispatch behavior is covered in more depth in the [Plumbing Dispatch Software: A 2026 Buying Guide](/guides/plumbing-dispatch-software/).

### What fills the reserved hours when no emergency comes in?

A standing release queue of work that has no fixed date: deferred drain maintenance, camera inspections, quoted jobs waiting on customer availability, warranty follow ups, and commercial preventive maintenance. Load it Monday with roughly twice the hours you reserved so a dispatcher never has to hunt for filler at release time.

### How do arrival windows work when an emergency lands?

The emergency gets a same day window rather than a fixed time, and every job it displaces should trigger an automatic text with the new window. Manual notification is where shops lose customers, because the dispatcher is busy handling the emergency at exactly the moment the moved customers need to hear from someone.

### Can a two person shop run reserved capacity?

Yes, at smaller scale. Two hours on one tech, released at midday, is enough structure to stop most cascades. The constraint is that with two techs a single long emergency consumes the whole hold, so your release queue and your subcontractor relationship both matter more than they do at eight techs.

Once your hold is running for a full month, the next lever is intake speed, since a reserved hour only helps if the emergency call reaches a dispatcher in the first place.
